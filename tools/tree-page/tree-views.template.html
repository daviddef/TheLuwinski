      Default navigator: Hourglass

      On every person page, with the person card beside it. Simple, never dead-ends, and the mini and upright modes cover phones and big families.

      Front door: Whole-tree map

      The landing page for a large GEDCOM. Shows the shape of the family and gets a visitor to a person in two clicks.

      The signature: Place lanes

      Nobody else has Atlas regimes behind a family timeline. This is the view that shows what is distinct about your sites, so put it on every line page.

      Story mode: Movements

      Use it as the animated opener for a family line, and for sharing. Fan stays as the print and poster view.

      How it ties into the Atlas

      Every place is a link out

      Birth and death places carry an Atlas pin map and a link to that place's Atlas page: the archives, the volumes, and the other names it has had.

      Pin colour = jurisdiction in that year

      The same place shows a different colour for a baptism in 1884 and one in 1930. This is the Atlas's own rule: it tells the visitor which archive to open.

      Gaps are honest

      Places the Atlas has no region for show a grey pin and an "add it" link. In this sample that is Mljet, Kraków, Warsaw, Stellenbosch and Perth.

      Events come from the Atlas, not from us

      The famine, epidemic and war bands use the Atlas mortality list, matched to each place by its outline. Nothing on this page is written by hand.

      For the real build: convert the GEDCOM once into a small JSON file, geocode each place name against the Atlas gazetteer (this is where its alternate names help), and draw everything in plain SVG. The map outlines and regime tables are already Atlas data, about 220 KB for what you see here. Withhold living people in the JSON itself; this mockup only hides their names. Movements need two places per person or a parent's place to compare, so an import report of "people with no place" is worth showing the researcher.
